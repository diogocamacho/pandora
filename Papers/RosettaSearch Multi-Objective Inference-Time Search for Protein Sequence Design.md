## Abstract

We introduce RosettaSearch, an inference-time multi-objective optimization approach for backbone conditioned protein sequence design. We use large language models (LLMs) as a generative optimizer within a search algorithm capable of controlled exploration and exploitation, using rewards computed from RosettaFold3, a structure prediction model, under a strict computational budget. In a large-scale evaluation, we apply RosettaSearch to 400 suboptimal sequences generated by LigandMPNN (a state-of-the-art model trained for protein sequence design), recovering high-fidelity designs that LigandMPNN's single-pass decoding fails to produce. RosettaSearch's designs show improvements in structural fidelity metrics ranging between 18% to 68%, translating to a 2.5x improvement in design success rate. We observe that these gains in success rate are robust when RosettaSearch-designed sequences are evaluated with an independent structure prediction oracle (Chai-1) and generalize across two distinct LLM families (o4-mini and Gemini-3), with performance scaling consistently with reasoning capability. We further demonstrate that RosettaSearch improves the sequence fidelity of ProteinMPNN designs for de novo backbones from the Dayhoff atlas, showing that the approach generalizes beyond native protein structures to computationally generated backbones. We also demonstrate a multi-modal extension of RosettaSearch with vision-language models, where images of predicted protein structures are used as feedback to incorporate structural context to guide protein sequence generation. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale demonstration that LLMs can serve as effective generative optimizers for backbone-conditioned protein sequence design, yielding systematic gains without any model retraining.

## The Challenge of Protein Sequence Fidelity

In the field of computational biology, protein sequence design—often called "inverse folding"—is the task of finding a specific sequence of amino acids that will reliably fold into a predetermined three-dimensional backbone structure. While recent deep learning architectures such as ProteinMPNN and LigandMPNN have significantly improved our ability to propose candidate sequences, they are essentially "single-pass" models. They generate a sequence in one go without a built-in mechanism to check if that sequence actually fits the target geometry.

The initial design from LigandMPNN (red) shows significant geometric deviation and low confidence compared to the native structure (blue). A major bottleneck in this process is "fidelity": even the best models often produce sequences that, when tested with independent structure prediction tools, fail to fold into the target shape or do so with very low confidence. This leads to a high failure rate in experimental lab settings. RosettaSearch addresses this by introducing an inference-time optimization loop. Instead of relying on a single prediction, it treats the sequence design task as a search problem, using Large Language Models (LLMs) to iteratively refine sequences based on structural feedback.

## The RosettaSearch Iterative Framework

The core of RosettaSearch is a feedback loop that connects sequence generation with structural evaluation. This approach recognizes that while LLMs may not have a perfect internal "physics engine" to predict protein folding, they are excellent at reasoning over complex feedback.

The workflow operates as follows:

1. **Initialization**: The process starts with a candidate sequence $s$ (typically from LigandMPNN) and a target backbone $x^*$.
2. **Structure Prediction (The Oracle)**: The sequence is passed to an "oracle"—in this case, RosettaFold3—which predicts the resulting 3D structure $\hat{x} = f(s)$.
3. **Metric Computation**: The predicted structure $\hat{x}$ is compared against the target $x^*$ using several geometric and confidence metrics.
4. **Feedback and Reasoning**: These metrics are converted into a structured text report. An LLM acts as the "optimizer," reading this report to understand which parts of the protein are folding poorly.
5. **Sequence Revision**: The LLM proposes a set of mutations to fix the identified issues, and the loop repeats.

The LLM optimizer receives a reward and textual feedback from the structural oracle and proposes a new sequence, which is then re-evaluated.*

## Quantifying Success: Multi-Objective Rewards

Designing a protein is rarely about optimizing a single number; it requires balancing multiple, often competing, objectives. RosettaSearch uses three primary metrics to guide the search:

- **$\text{pLDDT}$**: A confidence score (0–100) provided by the prediction model. A score above $70$ generally indicates a reliable local fold.
- **$\text{TM-score}$**: A measure (0–1) of global structural similarity.
- **$\text{C}_\alpha\text{-RMSD}$**: The average distance between the atoms of the predicted and target structures. Lower is better.

These are combined into a composite reward function $R(s)$ that the LLM tries to maximize:

$$
R(s) = w_{tm} \cdot \text{TM}(\hat{x}, x^*) + w_p \cdot \text{pLDDT}(\hat{x}) - w_r \cdot \text{RMSD}(\hat{x}, x^*)
$$

Where $w_{tm}$, $w_p$, and $w_r$ are weights that prioritize different aspects of structural fidelity. Crucially, the LLM does not just see this final number; it receives "local" feedback, such as: *"Residues 45-52 have high RMSD, suggesting a loop that is too flexible; consider stabilizing mutations."* This allows the model to perform targeted engineering rather than random guessing.

## Scaling the Search: Priority Search vs. Sequential Revision

While a simple "trial-and-error" (sequential) approach can work, RosettaSearch implements a more robust algorithm called **Priority Search**. In a sequential setup, the model might get stuck in a "local optimum"—a sequence that is better than the start but still not good enough.

Priority Search maintains a buffer of all evaluated sequences. In each iteration, it selects the top $K$ performing sequences and asks the LLM to generate $N$ new variations for each. This parallel exploration prevents the search from converging too early on a mediocre design. By managing a total budget of approximately $75$ evaluations per protein, the system can explore a wide variety of chemical substitutions while remaining computationally feasible.

## Results on Native and De Novo Backbones

The effectiveness of RosettaSearch was tested across several challenging datasets, including native PDB structures and synthetic (de novo) backbones generated by tools like RFDiffusion.

### Improving Success Rates

In a dataset of hundreds of monomeric proteins, RosettaSearch improved the "design success rate"—defined as achieving both a $\text{pLDDT}$ and $\text{TM-score}$ $\geq 0.8$ —by roughly 2.5 times compared to the LigandMPNN baseline. For sequences that initially failed to fold correctly, the LLM was able to find mutations that brought the predicted structure within $1.5\text{ \AA}$ of the target.

### Generalization Beyond the Oracle

A common concern in AI-driven optimization is "reward hacking," where the optimizer finds a way to fool the specific evaluation model (RosettaFold3) without actually improving the underlying protein. To test this, the researchers evaluated the final designs using a completely different model, Chai-1. The results held up: the relative improvements achieved during search were preserved, confirming that the LLM was learning genuine structural principles rather than model-specific quirks.

### De Novo Design

The system also excelled on the Dayhoff dataset, which contains backbones that do not exist in nature. Even without seeing a "native" sequence for reference, the LLM could use the structural feedback alone to refine ProteinMPNN designs, increasing the success rate from 72.4% to 89.5%.

## Reasoning and Visual Feedback

One of the unique contributions of this work is the exploration of multimodal feedback. By using Vision-Language Models (VLMs), the researchers provided the optimizer with rendered 3D images of the predicted structures. The VLMs demonstrated an ability to "see" spatial issues—like exposed hydrophobic patches or misaligned helices—and translate that visual information into specific amino acid changes.

Interestingly, while the LLM's reasoning was not always biologically perfect, its "chain-of-thought" process often correctly identified flexible terminal regions or poorly packed cores. This suggests that the LLM acts as a high-level reasoning engine that bridges the gap between raw geometric data and chemical intuition.

## Significance of the Work

RosettaSearch represents a significant shift in how we think about protein design. Traditionally, improving a model's performance meant collecting more data and retraining the entire network—a process that is slow and computationally expensive.

RosettaSearch demonstrates that we can achieve superior results through **inference-time refinement**. By using general-purpose LLMs as optimizers, researchers can:

1. **Avoid Retraining**: The same LLM can be used for different design tasks (enzymes, binders, or de novo structures) just by changing the text feedback.
2. **Handle Multi-Objective Trade-offs**: It is easy to add new constraints (like "do not mutate the binding site") by simply adding a sentence to the prompt.
3. **Bridge the Fidelity Gap**: It provides a reliable way to fix "near-miss" designs that are common in current state-of-the-art pipelines.

The method is highly adaptable; as LLMs become more capable of reasoning and protein structure models become faster, the efficiency and accuracy of RosettaSearch are expected to scale accordingly, making it a versatile tool for the next generation of protein engineering.
