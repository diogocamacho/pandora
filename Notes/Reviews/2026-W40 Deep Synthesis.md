# 2026-W40 — Deep Synthesis

## Evolutionary plausibility versus design fitness: the covariation trap in protein ML

The peptide binder saga of the past two weeks resolves into something more intellectually important than a priority dispute. The sequence runs like this: Anthropic publishes data showing Claude significantly outperforming human protein designers in de novo binder design, with an 80% hit rate on TREM2 against a 38% field baseline. Claus Wilke asks the obvious question -- what exactly did Claude do differently? Brandon Frenz answers it: Claude used ESMFold2 for scoring, while the field was using AlphaFold3 or inferior alternatives. Frenz further demonstrates that this choice was either deliberate (a knowledgeable decision encoded in the prompt) or accidental (AlphaFold3 licensing problems forcing a substitution), and that ESMFold2 is simply better calibrated for out-of-distribution sequences -- the non-evolutionary, computationally designed peptides that de novo binder work produces. The result: a $20,000 Claude workflow and a $200 direct ESMFold2 pipeline produce comparable quality outputs.

The cost story is a distraction. The deeper finding is about what the scoring function actually measures. Standard tools -- AlphaFold3, most pLMs used in design pipelines -- are trained predominantly on natural protein structures and sequences. They are excellent at evaluating whether a sequence is plausible given evolutionary history. De novo binder design is explicitly trying to build sequences that are not naturally occurring. ESMFold2 happens to be better calibrated for this regime, but the broader point is that the field has been using evolutionary plausibility as a proxy for designability, and these are different things. Evolutionary plausibility measures how well a sequence fits the statistical distribution that natural selection has sampled over billions of years. Designability measures whether a sequence folds stably into a conformation that binds a target. In well-studied protein families these correlate well; at the edges of sequence space they diverge, and de novo design lives at those edges.

Berry et al. make this mechanistically precise. Protein language models, trained to capture sequence covariation (the statistical dependencies between positions that reflect evolutionary pressure), do exactly what they were trained to do: they heavily penalize mutations that conflict with surrounding sequence context. This is appropriate for fitness prediction in evolved proteins. It is actively counterproductive for design tasks that require exploring sequence space that evolutionary history didn't visit. The finding Berry et al. report is counterintuitive but experimentally reproducible: a PSSM (position-specific amino acid frequencies from a multiple sequence alignment) minus the pLM score outperforms the pLM alone at identifying mutations that enable novel function. Subtracting the evolutionary model reveals the residual that evolutionary logic penalizes -- which turns out to be exactly where functional novelty lives. To find variants that generate or improve novel function, look for mutations that are common in MSAs (biochemically tolerated at a position) and that the pLM penalizes (rejected by context, absent from the evolutionary record). This is the inverse of standard practice.

Woolley et al. reach the same boundary from the fitness prediction side. Zero-shot predictions perform well in benchmarks because those benchmarks are built from mutations within the evolutionary distribution: single-point substitutions in well-characterized proteins, fitness landscapes where the ground truth is deep mutational scanning data reflecting natural sequence context. The benchmarks are internally consistent. The problem is that therapeutic design requires the opposite: large-effect mutations, combinations that would never appear in natural evolution, genuinely novel folds. Woolley's central empirical finding is that all available zero-shot models perform roughly equivalently, and that variance between datasets within models exceeds variance between models. This is the signature of a ceiling imposed by the task definition rather than model quality. No architectural improvement pushes a model past the point where its training distribution systematically differs from the target distribution.

For anyone running a protein design pipeline, this has specific operational implications. The canonical workflow -- generate candidates with RFdiffusion or ProteinMPNN, score with AlphaFold2/3 pLDDT and ipTM, filter by evolutionary plausibility -- is actively selecting against the mutations Berry et al. show are most likely to produce novel function. The workflow is not useless; it performs well for binders against structurally tractable targets with deep sequence databases. It fails in the cases that matter most for drug discovery: novel targets with limited structural context, therapeutically important mutations that break from natural sequence space, peptides designed against flat epitopes or intrinsically disordered regions. The BindCraft result Wilke reports -- roughly 1% experimental success rate in his lab despite published 10-100% success claims -- is what this failure mode looks like at the bench. The gap between published claim and realized performance is the gap between evolutionary plausibility and design fitness.

The Adaptyv hackathon sharpens this into a cautionary data point about AI agents specifically. Six agents (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT 5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4.1 Fast, Qwen 3.5 Plus, GLM 5) all independently converged on PXDesign as their design tool. The Adaptyv authors flag this as "tool monoculture." The more precise label is proxy monoculture: agents operating from the same publicly available benchmark results selected the same scoring functions, which encode the same evolutionary priors, which produce the same systematic blind spots. Human diversity in tool selection at least samples different regions of the prior distribution. Homogeneous agents produce homogeneous coverage of design space. If the canonical toolkit has a shared blind spot, no amount of agent parallelization finds what is in it.

## The bottleneck that AI doesn't reach: why Eroom's Law survives discovery optimization

Eroom's Law -- the number of new drugs per billion dollars of R&D spend halving roughly every nine years since 1950 -- has survived every wave of technology that was supposed to defeat it. It survived the biotech revolution, the Human Genome Project, combinatorial chemistry, and the first decade of AI drug discovery. The Formation Bio piece by Ben Liu and the Teslo and Scannell collaboration independently converge on the same structural explanation this week: the investment has consistently been hitting the wrong part of the pipeline.

Liu's data point is the most clarifying. Drug candidate discovery has roughly doubled over the past decade; FDA approvals have remained flat at approximately 50 per year. The pipeline doubled; the output didn't move. This is not a discovery bottleneck. It is a clinical development bottleneck. The binding constraint is patient recruitment, trial design, endpoint validation, regulatory strategy, and the organizational machinery required to run a Phase 2 or Phase 3 trial. ML has compressed early-stage prediction costs substantially. It has done almost nothing for any of these things. Teslo and Scannell's argument is that clinical development is where systematic optimization effort needs to go -- not as a complement to better discovery AI, but as the primary problem, because the returns to further discovery efficiency are now marginal while the returns to clinical development improvement remain high and largely untouched.

The China data serves as an empirical test of this argument. China's rise in biopharma -- from less than 5% of major pharmaceutical transactions worth $50 million or more in 2020 to nearly 30% in 2024 -- is real, structural, and well-documented. The NMPA's deliberate regulatory reform (ICH alignment, compressed review timelines), the return of US-trained scientists, and genuine cost advantages in Phase 1 and early Phase 2 trials are all contributing mechanisms. The Akeso ivonescimab result is a legitimate scientific achievement: a bispecific antibody outperforming Keytruda in head-to-head lung cancer data. But this is an improvement on a validated mechanism (PD-1/VEGF combination) in a well-characterized indication, executed in a high-volume trial system with lower per-patient costs. It is exactly what Eroom's Law analysis predicts Chinese competitive advantage should look like: a trial execution story, not a discovery story, because trial execution is where the real leverage has always resided.

The auto industry analogy in "Will all our drugs come from China?" is instructive but asymmetric in a way the article doesn't fully acknowledge. In automotive, the transition to electric vehicles changed the core competency so fundamentally that Chinese manufacturers could enter on near-equal technical footing. In pharma, modality transitions (small molecule to biologics to cell and gene therapy) have repeatedly changed discovery competencies -- but clinical trial design has remained structurally resistant to disruption across every transition. Trials are still enrolled patient by patient, randomized, blinded, and adjudicated by the same institutional machinery that ran them in 1980. Chinese trial efficiency comes from lower per-patient cost and faster enrollment in large patient populations, not from redesigning what a trial fundamentally is.

The "Uneven Frontiers" piece is worth reading as a company-building argument. Liu's analysis is that model improvements arrive faster than most companies can absorb them, so the product built on a capability that today seems proprietary becomes a commodity feature as soon as the frontier model release cycle catches up. The durable position is not "better discovery model" but "better execution of the constraint the model doesn't touch." This is Porter's operational effectiveness versus strategy distinction instantiated in biotechnology: anyone can run the model, but the trial organization is not something the model run contains. The companies that build around the constraint that doesn't move are the ones with the fastest, most operationally efficient path from candidate to trial readout -- which requires regulatory relationships, patient registries, adaptive trial design expertise, and a data flywheel that takes years to accumulate. The difficulty of building it is the moat.

## Cell identity as the molecular mechanism of aging

Two papers, Gladyshev et al. in Nature and Belmonte et al. in Cell, converge on a model of aging that revises one of the field's central assumptions. The damage accumulation model -- cells age because reactive oxygen species, DNA double-strand breaks, protein aggregates, and mitochondrial dysfunction accumulate over time -- is not wrong. Both papers are explicit about this. But it is incomplete in a way that has practical consequences for intervention design, and the incompleteness is directional: the damage model points at different therapeutic targets than the identity-loss model.

The new model: aging is primarily a loss of cell identity. Specialized cells maintain their identity through a three-tier epigenetic regulatory grammar. The fast tier -- transcription factors like AP-1 -- responds to acute stress in minutes to hours. The intermediate tier governs cell state transitions over days to weeks, cycling cells through activated and basal states in response to tissue demands. The slow tier is chromatin architecture itself: nucleosome positioning, histone modifications, DNA methylation, and three-dimensional genome folding, which insulates and locks the cell's identity against perturbation by the fast and intermediate tiers. The slow tier deteriorates asymmetrically with age. The fast and intermediate tiers can still respond, but without the chromatin scaffolding that defines what "home" looks like, the responses don't return the cell to the same identity state. The cell drifts.

The consequence is mesenchymal shift: cells across organ types gradually acquire fibroblast-like characteristics -- inflammatory cytokine secretion, extracellular matrix deposition, reduced specialized function. This is not random thermal-noise damage; it is directed drift toward a generic attractor that happens to be the most evolutionarily conserved cell program. Waddington's epigenetic landscape, proposed in 1957, maps precisely onto this: the valleys that represent stable cell identities are maintained by active canalization, and aging shallows those valleys. The cell doesn't lose all function; it loses its identity-specific function while retaining generic cellular survival behaviors. The phenotype of aged tissue -- fibrosis, inflammation, reduced functional reserve -- is the phenotype of cells that have drifted into a generic mesenchymal attractor state.

For T-cell exhaustion specifically, this reframing is mechanistically useful rather than merely descriptive. Exhaustion has been treated as a distinct epigenetic state with a characteristic transcription factor program (TOX, NR4A1, BATF) caused by chronic antigen stimulation and enforced by Polycomb repressive complexes locking the exhausted state. The Gladyshev/Belmonte framework suggests an overlapping mechanism: exhausted T cells may be exhibiting the same Waddington decanalization that aged cells show in other tissues, with chromatin architecture drifting away from effector identity toward a more generic, mesenchymal-adjacent low-energy state. Under this reading, PRC2 inhibition restores T-cell effector function not primarily by overriding the exhaustion transcription factor program, but by restoring chromatin accessibility to the effector loci that decanalization had made inaccessible. The intervention target is the slow tier, not the fast one. This distinction matters for therapeutic design because it suggests the window for effective intervention is governed by how far chromatin architecture has drifted -- which may not map cleanly onto the transcription factor state that canonical exhaustion markers capture.

## The connective tissue

All three threads this week share a structure: a proxy measure that is internally consistent, correctly predicts outcomes within the domain where it was calibrated, and systematically misleads about the cases where the real leverage is. PLMs measure evolutionary plausibility accurately within natural protein space; this makes them poor guides for design tasks at the edges of that space. ML drug discovery measures early-stage prediction accuracy and improves it continuously; this leaves the expensive, high-variance stages of clinical development untouched, which is where Eroom's Law actually operates. The damage model of aging accurately captures discrete molecular insults and some chronic disease processes; it misses the identity-loss mechanism that Gladyshev and Belmonte argue is a primary driver of the aging phenotype.

The common failure mode is not that the proxy is wrong. It is that the proxy was calibrated in one domain and applied without modification to a different domain where the underlying assumptions break down. Evolutionary plausibility is an excellent proxy for natural protein fitness; it is a systematically biased prior for de novo design. Early-stage molecular validation reliably predicts candidate quality given that clinical development proceeds the way it always has; it says nothing about the bottleneck preventing improved clinical productivity. Damage accumulation is a real mechanism; it doesn't account for the coordinated epigenetic drift that the new aging papers describe.

The common escape is not a better proxy. It is a second measurement that reveals what the first one misses. Berry et al.'s PSSM-minus-pLM is a residual: what remains after subtracting what evolutionary logic predicts. The Formation Bio analysis adds a second data track: approved drugs alongside discovered candidates, which reveals the divergence that discovery metrics alone conceal. The aging papers add cell identity measurements alongside damage assays, surfacing the mechanism the damage assays were blind to. In each case, the scientific advance came from adding a measurement orthogonal to the primary proxy, probing the dimension the primary measurement was implicitly assuming away.

## What this shifts

The Berry et al. result is immediately actionable for protein design work. Ranking candidates by pLM score -- filtering by evolutionary plausibility -- is selecting against the mutations most likely to produce genuinely novel function. The practical alternative is stratification: maintain a subset that the pLM penalizes but that appear in MSA at meaningful frequency. That is where Berry et al. find the novel-function candidates. This changes experimental triage in any design campaign where the goal is functional novelty rather than stabilization or optimization within an existing scaffold.

The Formation Bio/Teslo/Scannell convergence argues something specific about where durable value accumulates. The discovery AI position is being commoditized in real time; ESMFold2 is open source, RFdiffusion is open source, and the Anthropic experiment showed that a well-written prompt with the right scoring function matches bespoke pipelines. Clinical development is not being commoditized by the current generation of AI. The companies that build around the constraint that doesn't move are not the ones with the best discovery model; they are the ones with the fastest, most operationally efficient path from candidate to trial readout. That requires regulatory relationships, patient registries, adaptive trial design expertise, and a proprietary data flywheel. The difficulty of building it is the moat.

## Bridges

The Berry et al. PSSM-minus-pLM finding has a structural twin in quantitative finance: alpha extraction from factor-adjusted equity returns. A factor model (market beta, size, value, momentum) captures what market logic expects from a stock. Subtracting the factor model yields the residual -- idiosyncratic return that systematic factors don't explain. The most interesting trades historically lived in that residual, not in the factor exposures that everyone could measure and therefore everyone had already priced. Berry et al. found the protein design equivalent: evolutionary priors play the role of the factor model, capturing what evolutionary logic expects of a sequence. The alpha -- the mutations that biochemistry tolerates but evolution rejected -- lives in the residual. Protein design has been running a crowded factor trade, and the alpha was in the residual the whole time.

The Waddington basin metaphor for cellular identity has an analogue in tonal harmony that makes the aging mechanism concrete. Tonal music operates under a grammar: rules about which transitions are stable, which are dissonant, and how dissonances resolve. When the grammar is intact, even remote harmonic regions resolve predictably back to the tonic. When the grammar erodes -- as happens in late Romantic chromaticism when the tonal center is overwhelmed -- notes that should resolve no longer do. Themes stated in one key cannot find their way home. The Gladyshev/Belmonte finding describes the same process in cellular terms: the chromatin architecture that constitutes the cell's harmonic grammar erodes with age, dissonant gene expression patterns no longer resolve to the cell-type-specific state, and the cell drifts toward a generalized low-energy attractor. The aging cell is a tonal system that has lost its center of gravity -- which implies that identity maintenance is an active, ongoing process, not a passive equilibrium that damage disrupts.

The experience-over-skills data from Revelio Labs, read alongside Berry and Woolley, clarifies what is actually scarce in computational biology right now. The skills being most rapidly commoditized by AI are exactly the ones that seemed hardest to acquire a decade ago: model training, benchmark evaluation, standard assay pipeline design. What is not commoditized is the ability to recognize when a benchmark is measuring the wrong thing -- to have internalized enough failure modes, across enough distinct biological systems, to treat the BindCraft 10-100% success claim with appropriate skepticism before running 200 designs. That capacity comes from years of being misled by well-validated proxies and then finding out at the bench that the validation didn't transfer. The experience premium Revelio is measuring is real, and the reason it is rising is specific: anyone can run the model, but knowing which model is measuring the wrong thing requires a long history of being fooled by proxy validity.
