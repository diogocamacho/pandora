- **China pharma is a structural story, not a headline.** Three pieces this week converge on the same point: China's drug industry rise is driven by regulatory maturation and capital efficiency, not just cheap labor — and Western firms are still treating it as a threat rather than a signal about their own cost structures.
- **The peptide-binder debate reveals a methods problem.** The Anthropic/biohub back-and-forth (Claus Wilke's posts) isn't really about who "solved" binder design — it's about what counts as solved when benchmarks and real-world utility diverge. The field is measuring the wrong thing.
- **Protein language models have a covariation trap.** PLMs trained to capture evolutionary covariation do well on fitness prediction but poorly on design tasks that require breaking from natural sequence space — which is exactly where drug discovery needs them most.
- **Zero-shot mutational effect predictions are useful until they aren't.** The Wilke piece on zero-shot predictions lands on a sharp caveat: performance degrades precisely in the high-stakes cases (large-effect mutations, novel folds), which are the cases that matter for therapeutic design.
- **Drug discovery is optimizing the wrong funnel stage.** Teslo's piece on human trials and the "DeepSeek moment" article both point to the same structural issue: the industry is pouring resources into early-stage prediction while late-stage trial design remains broken.
