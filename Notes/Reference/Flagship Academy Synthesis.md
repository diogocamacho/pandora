## The Flagship frame in one sentence

Discover a generative platform in unprecedented terrain, form a company around it, validate it step by step, and extract multiple products from a single foundational investment.

---

## What pioneering actually means

A pioneering species is the first to enter a barren environment — no path, no map, no guarantee it's safe. Flagship uses this deliberately. Pioneering is a **willful choice to start from scratch** in spaces where existing systems don't apply, which often means redesigning the entire value chain around your idea.

The opposite of pioneering is working in the **adjacency zone** — where experts can assess feasibility, competitors already exist, and the upside is incremental. Most biotech lives here. Flagship bets it doesn't have to.

---

## The three pioneering mindsets

**Future-back thinking** — Start with a bold, specific vision of the future and work backward. Never describe your innovation by comparing it to what exists today (the horse-drawn carriage mistake). Take your audience to the destination first, then let them contrast with the present if they need to.

**Unreasonable** — Pursue hypotheses that can't be reasoned from today's knowledge. A reasonable hypothesis can be measured because it's adjacent. An unreasonable one requires a leap of faith. The test: if you can assess risk/benefit on a normal scale, it's probably not unreasonable enough. Don't stack unreasonable on top of unreasonable — one leap per hypothesis.

**Consumer of uncertainty** — Uncertainty isn't risk to be avoided; it's raw material to be transformed into *manageable* risk through exploration. The greatest opportunities live outside the adjacency zone: no competitors, ability to define the space, and by definition the platform is novel.

## How Flagship builds companies

Four phases, not linear — science and commercial thinking run in parallel throughout:

1. **Explorations** — "What if...?" hypotheses tested in Flagship Labs. 80–100 per year. Evolve through variation and selection, kill what doesn't survive contact with reality.
2. **ProtoCos (FL###)** — Promising explorations get a number and a founding team. Test the core science. Discontinue if it can't validate. 8–10 originations per year.
3. **NewCos** — "It turns out..." replaces "What if...". Full capital commitment, name, CEO, board. Platform development in earnest. 6–8 per year.
4. **GrowthCos** — External investors, partnerships, IPO path. 25 IPOs since 2013.

Timing: engage with potential partners early. Not to sell, but to get market insight that shapes the platform's evolution. Over-refining in isolation is a trap.

## What makes a platform

A platform is **a set of scientific principles, methods, tools, and technologies that, when integrated, generate multiple product options**. Not a single technology — a system.

The key differentiator is **programmability**: how easily the platform adapts across product opportunities with small changes. Nucleic acids = high programmability (change a sequence, get a new product). Microbiome = low programmability (each product requires reconfiguration). Higher programmability → more options at lower marginal cost → higher option value.

**FAB framework** for communicating platform value:
- **F** — Technical capabilities of the platform
- **A** — Why it's better than existing approaches
- **B** — What specific benefits the advantages confer, and to whom

**Value formula**: `value = (size of opportunity) × (probability of success)`

Map value hypotheses early. The intersection of *important × wow × easy* is where you sequence first.

**Platformability test** — Ask of any idea:
- Generative? (Can it produce multiple outputs?)
- Programmable? (Can it be adapted cheaply?)
- Reusable? (Does the core hold across applications?)
- "Where can you go from here?" — island, archipelago, or tip of a continent?

## Common platform company traps

1. **Premature scaling** — investing across multiple product candidates before the core platform is validated. You may run out of money before proving anything.
2. **Fixating on the hardest problem** — difficulty ≠ value. Hard problems that aren't valuable still drain resources.
3. **Failure to update assumptions** — distinguish facts from assumptions and update when data arrives.
4. **Single-asset thinking on a platform** — losing the broader vision by over-investing in one product concept.

The counter: **sequence investments**. Validate pieces of the platform iteratively, not all at once. Show the platform creates multiple viable options — that's what demonstrates it's a platform, not a lucky product.

## Drug discovery fundamentals

**What makes a good target?**
- Druggability (new modalities opened many structures previously unreachable)
- Selectivity
- Biomarker availability
- Human genetic validation (GWAS, transcriptomics, somatic mutations, clinical observations)

**Target validation** requires demonstrating causality, selectivity, and translational relevance.

**TPP (Target Product Profile)** defines: patient population, pharmacodynamics, biodistribution, efficacy, safety/tolerability, route of administration. Everything in development maps back to it.

**Pre-clinical**: ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion) drives human dosing. Exposure drives safety evaluation — track unbound concentration, not just total drug. Toxicology in pre-clinical is **expected and informative**, not a failure. If you don't see tox, ask why.

Platform success in drug discovery depends on **where you aim the platform** — right diseases, right targets, right mechanism + modality.

## IP fundamentals

**A patent is a limited monopoly** exchanged for public disclosure. It gives you the right to exclude — nothing more.

**Scope is defined entirely by the claims** — the sentences at the end, negotiated with the patent office. Everything else enables and supports them.

**Patentability requirements**: Patentable subject matter · Novelty · Non-obviousness · Enablement · Written description · Definiteness

**Timeline**: File provisional (month 0) → 12 months to beef it up → PCT at 18 months (becomes prior art for others and yourself) → granted patent 2–5 years after entering national phase.

**Tech vs discovery company IP strategy**:
- **Tech company** (e.g., modality like mRNA): file broad foundational patents early. The platform itself can be described and claimed. Picket-fence approach.
- **Discovery company** (e.g., computational platform): platform is hard to police. Rely on trade secrets for the platform itself; file on the *outputs* (specific product concepts). "Have your cake and eat it too."

**Exclusivity vs FTO**: You can have strong patents (exclusivity) while still lacking freedom to operate if others hold blocking patents on essential enabling technology. Both need active management.

## Financing basics

Core questions: When? How much? What type? From whom?

**Debt vs equity**:
- Debt avoids dilution but creates repayment risk and potential conversion clauses
- Equity gives up control in exchange for capital

**NPV vs ROV**: Flagship uses discount rates for *company* valuation and probability-weighted models for *programs*. Real option valuation (ROV) is essentially a decision tree — useful for sequencing investment decisions.

**Partnering is always dilutive** — treat it as equity on a project. Strategic partnerships provide capital and validation, but you're giving up a share of project value.

Financing is one component of overall strategy alongside: platform · partnering · pipeline · capabilities.
