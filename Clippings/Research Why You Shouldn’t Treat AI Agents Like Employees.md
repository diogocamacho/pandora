As organizations push to accelerate AI adoption, many leaders are considering a simple idea: Stop treating AI as a tool and start treating it as an employee.

Agentic AI systems make this framing feel more plausible. These systems operate with greater autonomy and increasingly can execute some tasks on par with humans. Leaders assume that anthropomorphizing AI will make the technology feel less foreign to workers or that it will signal the company’s AI ambitions to investors, customers, or internal stakeholders.

But it turns out that treating AI as an employee is not so straightforward. In a randomized [experiment](https://emmawiles.github.io/storage/ai_employee.pdf), we found that humanizing AI can shift accountability away from individuals, increase escalation, reduce review quality, and erode professional identity and trust. What’s more, it doesn’t meaningfully increase people’s intent to adopt the technology and integrate it into workflows—which remain the key obstacle to capturing AI’s enormous value creation promise.

These findings highlight a critical point: Agentic AI has significant potential to expand what organizations can do and how work gets done. The challenge is not whether to adopt it, but how to integrate it into workflows in ways that preserve accountability, maintain quality, and enable employees to work effectively alongside it.

## AI Employees Enter the Workforce

Organizations are formalizing the role of AI agents in a variety of ways. For example, they are giving them names and job titles, listing them on an org or work chart, assigning them managers, or referring to them as teammates or employees. [Several CEO announcements](https://www.wsj.com/articles/digital-workers-have-arrived-in-banking-bf62be49) during the last year have brought these “AI or digital employees” into broader discussions of AI adoption. We’ve also heard from executives exploring the idea, from casual mentions of AI colleagues to formal announcements of adding these colleagues to the org chart.

Some of these framing choices are social and symbolic. Giving AI a human name, for example, gives people a way to address the system and, in some cases, can make it seem more familiar or approachable. Others are governance choices that trigger a set of expectations about authority, accountability, and oversight that the organization applies to its other employees. Whatever the specific practice, each reflects the same broader impulse—moving AI from something employees use to something the organization recognizes as a member of the team in its own right.

Our own study suggests this shift is already underway. In fact, among the 1,261 managers, directors, and executives (hereafter, “managers”) in HR and finance from the U.S., Canada, and the European Union who participated in our experiment, 31% said their company’s leadership already frames AI as a teammate or employee, and 23% said that their organization has AI agents listed on its org or work charts. This was true beyond the technology sector; we found the practice extends into industries such as healthcare, financial services, retail, and professional services.

Among our experiment participants, one organization’s head of people told us about “Scout,” an AI agent that is formally listed on the company’s HR team org chart. Scout is responsible for reviewing job applications, conducting first-round interviews, and then putting forth candidates, each with an evaluation summary. Scout isn’t a generic chatbot that recruiters prompt periodically. Instead, Scout functions like a junior employee, acting autonomously with a defined scope, outputs, and boundaries—embedded directly into the company’s hiring process workflow.

When describing Scout, the HR leader notes that “it’s technically an equivalent peer on your team. That’s how it acts and behaves.” This is where the framing starts to get murky—and provides a call to action: Leaders are moving ahead with the “AI employee” faster than they understand its impact on employees and organizations.

## The Effects of Humanizing AI

In our experiment, we asked managers to review workplace documents that contained errors. The only thing we changed among our three groups was the identification of the original drafter: an AI tool, a human employee on their team (“Alex”), or an AI employee on their team (“ALEX-3”). This framing difference did not significantly impact behavior of managers unfamiliar with AI employees. But when we focused our analysis on participants whose organizations already have AI agents on their org or work charts—that is, managers with some level of real-world exposure to AI agents—the findings pointed to a number of challenges that require work and governance redesign.

### Accountability becomes blurred.

We asked participants who they held accountable for the documents they reviewed—and to allocate responsibility among themselves, their team, the AI system, and leadership. When AI was framed as an employee rather than as a tool, personal accountability fell by 9 percentage points, while accountability attributed to the AI rose by 8 percentage points. This is problematic, because today’s AI systems cannot be held accountable and require clear human ownership.

In one interview, a participant whose company lists “Kevin” as an AI employee on the org chart described his colleagues joking, “We’re working with Kevin… he’s a little dry,” treating the AI as a social actor. When errors occur, the framing shifts to, “Hey, Kevin’s making a mistake. Why is he making that mistake?” By narrating failures as “Kevin’s mistake,” responsibility is redirected to the AI rather than to the humans who deployed, supervised, or approved its output—blurring accountability.

Moreover, when responsibility is reassigned to the AI and diffused across the team, it becomes easier for humans to shirk responsibility for shortcomings in the agent’s output. For example, when asked who is to blame for errors in AI-generated work in their organization, one participant explained: “The blame isn’t on a person; it’s on the technology.”

### Escalation and the burden on others increase.

Participants were given the option to escalate the documents they had checked for errors to a manager for an additional review, with incentives designed to reflect real workplace tradeoffs between confidence in one’s work and the cost of passing work on for further review. Compared with the AI tool framing, the AI employee framing increased requests for additional review by 44%.

Additional review of AI output is not inherently problematic, particularly in high-stakes or regulated environments where oversight is essential. The issue arises when additional review is used as a substitute for one’s own careful checking, rather than as part of a defined governance process. In our study, those checking the work of an AI employee had a comparatively lower perception of their own judgment, given those participants were more likely to pass work onward rather than stand behind their review.

In fact, open-ended responses suggest that AI involvement reduced confidence in reviewers’ ability to catch all errors. Several participants explicitly referenced questioning their own skills, doubting whether they had identified all issues, or feeling the need to re-verify work they would typically accept at face value. This represents an additional cost to organizations through extra review cycles, specifically when AI is an employee rather than tool.

### Quality control declines.

In our experiment, we included errors in the documents that managers are expected to catch, such as inconsistent information, factual inaccuracies, and incorrect calculations, and asked participants to identify as many errors as possible within a time limit. We crafted performance-based incentives to encourage careful review.

Compared with the AI tool framing, participants that reviewed documents with the AI employee framing caught 18% fewer errors. Managers in the AI employee group were more likely to miss important inconsistencies or flawed logic. For example, in one budget document, a contract was said to reduce costs, but the accompanying spreadsheet showed an increase in total expenses. In another case, a job description for an entry-level role required more than 10 years of experience in the field. These types of errors were more likely to go undetected when managers were told they came from an AI employee than from an AI tool.

In another recent study, [we defined “AI brain fry”](https://hbr.org/2026/03/when-using-ai-leads-to-brain-fry) as mental fatigue from excessive use or oversight of AI tools beyond one’s cognitive capacity. Workers experiencing brain fry reported making mistakes significantly more often—scoring 11% and 39% higher on the minor and major error frequency measures, respectively—than those who did not. In this context, AI employee framing may introduce a related risk: When managers review AI as a tool, they still experience the cognitive burden of oversight. When the same output is framed as coming from an “employee,” managers may feel less need to fully engage in the cognitive burden of review—effectively absolving some of that burden—and apply less scrutiny to catching errors.

### Professional identity and trust take a hit.

Among our whole sample, managers were 13% more likely to report uncertainty about their professional identity when their companies’ leadership framed AI as a teammate or employee rather than as a productivity tool. Managers in companies using the anthropomorphizing framing also reported a 7% higher concern about job security and a 10% lower level of trust in how AI would be used in the organization.

These concerns were echoed in follow-up conversations, where participants felt the uncertainty acutely. As one participant summarized bluntly: “If you want people to feel like they will lose their job to AI, or can be easily replaced by AI, then put it on the org chart.” Another participant noted that the AI as employee framing “makes you wonder what your role is supposed to be when AI is treated like a colleague.” Employees are navigating new AI colleagues without role clarity from their employers as many companies have yet to clarify what success looks like in roles with AI, redefine responsibilities, and implement new performance management standards.

This effect may seem obvious given uncertainty across the labor market, but it’s a leadership issue. In companies that push AI without addressing their human employees’ go-forward role, managers may assume human roles are universally becoming less relevant. Yet AI transformations are not monolithic. Some roles are substitutable; many others are augmentable, intended to amplify human judgment and open-ended problem-solving. When companies fail to distinguish between the two, employees are left to interpret AI solely as a substitute rather than a support, undermining trust and culture for all adopting populations.

### Adoption does not meaningfully increase.

Despite common assumptions, our data shows that humanizing AI doesn’t increase intent to adopt. Participants exposed to the AI employee framing did not report higher adoption intent than those exposed to the AI tool framing, and participants from organizations that framed AI as an employee or teammate showed no clear difference in AI adoption compared with those whose organizations framed AI as a tool.

In practice, we found that adoption followed managerial encouragement and expectations more than framing. As one manager noted, “At the point that I saw it was becoming tied to employee success — when somebody used an LLM, they got featured at a town hall — I started telling everybody on my team, ‘You’ve got to use this as much as you can.’” This pattern is consistent with a recent [BCG study](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/ai-transformation-is-a-workforce-transformation) on AI and workforce transformation: Companies leading in AI maturity are 3.5 times more likely to have managers who actively role-model AI use in daily operations**,** underscoring that visible managerial signaling drives adoption.

## Redesigning Work for Responsible Human-AI Collaboration

Our research suggests that while AI governance is always an imperative, it becomes even more critical when AI is anthropomorphized. As AI increases speed and volume, organizations cannot simply layer it onto workflows built for human pace. Based on our experience as economists studying AI’s impact on organizations and labor markets and advisors to more than 400 companies undergoing AI transformations over the last three years, we believe that CEOs need to redesign the work of both AI and humans in five key ways:

### 1\. Explicitly redefine workflows and then name new (human) role expectations.

As AI agents take on execution, human roles will increasingly focus on supervision, judgment, relationship building, and managing ambiguity. In our work with clients, we find this shift in accountability is going unsaid in many workplaces today while AI native companies build this into their talent model explicitly. As workflows change, [so do human roles](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/ai-will-reshape-more-jobs-than-it-replaces), requiring new skills that come with new expectations. Leaders must:

- **Create the right sphere of accountability and span of control.** Oversight capacity does not expand automatically just because output does. Leaders need to redesign spans of control and team size with that in mind.
- **Redesign roles at scale and clarify expectations.** Explicitly state oversight responsibility over AI systems in job descriptions, set realistic expectations for velocity and volume of work, and name the persistent human skills that remain critical.
- **Reset performance management.** Reward the quality of oversight and effective orchestration of AI system, not just speed and output.

### 2\. Make accountability explicit and personal for employees and agents.

The evidence presented in our study suggests that AI agents should be treated as what they are—software automation that requires clear human accountability for the output and operation. When AI contributes to an outcome, it should be made salient to the responsible humans that are accountable for it. This is especially critical in highly regulated environments.

This requires clear rules of collaboration across humans and agents, grounded in shared objectives and accountability over time**,** across three fronts:

- **Decision rights:** Because agents can handle far more flexible situations than traditional automation, it is critical to define what the agent is allowed to do autonomously vs. what requires explicit human approval.
- **Escalation:** What triggers review? Who intervenes? Who bears the cost of delay or error?
- **Consequences:** When the agent fails, what happens next? Define how accountability is carried forward over time. Accountable human employees are responsible for monitoring agent performance and providing ongoing improvement.

### 3\. Design and implement a capability building plan for employees managing agents.

The ability to oversee human-agentic systems does not always organically appear, as evidenced by firms only achieving value from AI when they [protect employee learning](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/what-ceos-should-look-for-in-an-ai-first-chief-people-officer?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=ceo-agenda&utm_description=organic&utm_topic=ai&utm_geo=global&utm_content=global_linkedin_exhibit&linkId=874184034). Organizations need a new managerial toolkit and training curriculum to help employees navigate a different balance between delegation and control when working with AI. Employees must understand the [full range of tasks AI can perform](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2023/how-people-create-and-destroy-value-with-gen-ai), use it as a source of intelligence rather than just task execution, and integrate it into workflows in ways that improve decisions. Just as important, they must know when to rely on AI, when to challenge its outputs, and where its limitations differ from those of human workers.

### 4\. Don’t constrain agents into 1-for-1 roles.

The concept of “employee” assumes bounded roles, finite human capacity, and a hierarchy in which work is delegated to someone less experienced or knowledgeable. [AI does not share these limits](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/the-ai-first-life-insurance-company). A single agent can operate across many workflows; multiple agents can reshape one job. Framing agents as one-to-one human equivalents reinforces a delegation mindset and underestimates their capabilities, pushing companies toward like-for-like replacement and limiting how much value a reimagined system can create. Leaders should instead design the right agentic unit for the workflow, which may be a broader functional capability used across a team or process, rather than defaulting to one agent per human role.

### 5\. Make deliberate choices about how human work evolves.

At the end of the day, companies will still need human employees to work with their AI agents, regardless of whether it is framed as a tool or an “employee.” But simply asking employees to use AI to “do more” is not a recipe for building positive engagement or bolstering professional identity. Prior [BCG Henderson Institute research](https://hbr.org/2025/11/leaders-assume-employees-are-excited-about-ai-theyre-wrong) highlights a significant perception gap: While 76% of executives believe employees feel enthusiastic about AI adoption, only 31% of individual contributors report the same.

In an agentic system, how work is divided between humans and AI is a design choice. As AI improves productivity, it creates the opportunity to redeploy human effort toward higher-value activities where demand is growing. Realizing that opportunity depends on deliberate role design, ensuring that human effort is focused on work that requires judgment, creativity, and ownership—areas that both drive value and reinforce positive engagement.

### ...

As AI usage scales across organizations, even small declines in review rigor or uncertainty over ownership compound. Output increases—but so does the number and cost of mistakes. The companies that capture disproportionate value will be those that redesign work, operating models and workforce capabilities to mitigate these risks, enabling AI to expand contribution as humans deepen judgment, accountability, and impact.
