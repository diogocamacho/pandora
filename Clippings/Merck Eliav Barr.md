“The docs told me it’s a different type of medicine,” said the architect. “They were right,” he added with a smile.

Sitting in my clinic exam room, Bill, an 86-year-old practicing architect, was the picture of health. A mop of curly white hair framed sharp features, thick, black-rimmed glasses, and tan skin, leathered by harsh Cape Cod sun. Four years ago, he was diagnosed with metastatic melanoma, the same cancer that killed his wife two decades earlier. “After she passed, I swore I would never do chemo,” he said, “but my doctors convinced me to try immunotherapy.” I glanced at his chart; the drug he took was *Keytruda* – developed by Merck and approved in 2014.

What does it take to make a different type of medicine? How do you validate emerging science, design the right trials, and ultimately win FDA approval? According to Dr. Eliav Barr, Head of Global Clinical Development and Chief Medical Officer of Merck, the answer is simple: tell a compelling story. “\[Drug development\] is a million little things, constantly. You have to tell a good story, motivate people and keep it simple,” he says.

Merck has a long track record of impactful medicines: the first statin, vaccines like Gardasil, household names like Fosamax, Singulair and immunotherapies like Keytruda (the drug that saved Bill’s life). Its current pipeline is even more daring: WINREVAIR for pulmonary arterial hypertension, tulisokibart for IBD, sac-TMT (a TROP2 ADC) for solid tumors and a personalized cancer vaccine (with Moderna), all aimed at diseases long considered intractable.

Dr. Barr’s job is to shepherd these medicines and vaccines through dozens of concurrent trials to patients. It is a difficult job. Early on, after moving into vaccines, he nearly quit: “It was such a new area for me, I called all of my mentors and asked, ‘Oh my god, what am I doing? Maybe I should leave.’” One mentor reminded him: “When you came to Merck, you didn’t come here to just be a cardiologist. You came to develop medicines that improve public health and extend people’s lives around the world.”

A cardiologist by training, a young Eliav earned his MD at Thomas Jefferson University at just 22, completed residency and cardiology fellowship at Johns Hopkins, and did postdoctoral gene therapy work in Jeff Leiden’s lab at the University of Michigan. After a short stint on the faculty at the University of Chicago, he joined Merck in 1995. In 1998, he took over the HPV vaccine program that would become *Gardasil 9*. He later led Merck’s infectious disease clinical development and global medical affairs, and in 2022 succeeded Roy Baynes as chief medical officer.

In drug development, it is easy to lose the forest for the trees. Novel molecular mechanisms, medicinal chemistry, GLP tox, target engagement biomarkers, trial design, NDA/BLA filings: each step is lengthy, complex, and rife with failure.

Yet each of these “million little things” can make the difference between health and sickness, between Bill’s fate and that of his wife. Dr. Barr hasn’t seen a patient in clinic for some time, but by helping guide drugs to market, he and his colleagues at Merck shape the lives of millions.

On his way out, Bill told me he is moving closer to his daughter and starting work on a hospital design contract. As Eliav Barr reminds us: every good drug needs a good story, and thanks to a “million little things” going right for *Keytruda*, Bill’s is still being written.

**1) Who were your early mentors who pushed you towards science and medicine?**

> I come from a medical family, an immigrant medical family. My father was a cardiologist, and my mother was an ICU nurse, so I was constantly exposed to medicine. Going into the profession came easily in a sense. My father chose to be a cardiologist, built an amazing practice, and spent his time on the ground with patients. I wanted to do R&D, though I didn’t know what that meant at the time.
> 
> At Jefferson Medical College, I worked closely with Willis Maddrey, a hepatologist who was chief of the Department of Medicine and a very big name in drug toxicity and drug-induced hepatitis. I learned a lot from him, and he told me to go to Hopkins, where he had trained and done most of his formative clinical work. So I went to Hopkins. It was the middle of the AIDS epidemic: the horrors of the disease, extraordinary stress on the system, and some very difficult behavior from institutions. I was really into infectious disease, but my dad wanted me to become a cardiologist, so I did.
> 
> I’d gone through a five-year combined premed and medical program at Penn State and Jefferson. It was crazy. I finished medical school at 22, was then a resident and a fellow at 26, and I thought, “What’s the rush?” So, after my cardiology fellowship, I decided to learn what research was like.
> 
> Earlier, I did a rotation in the CCU at the Brigham. My dad, who trained at Harvard, helped arrange this. My mentor there was Jeff Leiden, a great researcher who was heading to Ann Arbor, and I went to work with him at the University of Michigan. Jeff later became a senior leader at Abbott and then CEO of Vertex, a very big name in drug development and cardiovascular medicine. He gave me a spot in his lab doing gene therapy work around 1990.
> 
> That same Howard Hughes group included Francis Collins, who went on to lead the NIH; Craig Thompson, a major B cell biologist who became president of Memorial Sloan Kettering; and Gary Nabel and his wife Betsy, who led the NHLBI and then became president of the Brigham. These people were crazy amazing, and I got to do a lot of interesting work. A big part of the group then moved to the University of Chicago, and later to Penn. I left them at Chicago because I’d realized I no longer wanted to do bench research. Instead, I wanted to move to human research.
> 
> Jeff was a mentor, a taskmaster, and a teacher. He was a very tough guy, and he taught me the scientific method: how to write a grant, how to tell a story and why storytelling matters for getting ideas across, how to be logical, objective, and well supported. He cared about sentence structure, proper experiments, and precision. He worked like a dog. It was tough, but it was formative.

**2) What led you to industry, and why Merck in particular?**

> I joined Merck in 1995 because in the mid-90s it had done some of the most important cardiovascular work of the era. Merck showed that statins save lives. Before that, cholesterol lowering wasn’t a key part of cardiovascular prevention, and Merck ran the first studies. With CONSENSUS, Merck also showed that ACE inhibitors save lives in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, the classic heart failure. They were running these mega trials, and I thought, “Ooh, that’s cool, I’d like to do that.” So I jumped to Merck, which turned out to be when cardiovascular drug development started to slow down.
> 
> Maybe I’m attracted to tough, demanding leaders. My next mentor was Reynold Spector. He held the job I have now, chief medical officer and head of global development, and he taught me clinical pharmacology. He emphasized why drug concentration, absorption, distribution, and metabolism matter, and how they can make or break a drug. Further, what target engagement means and how to measure pharmacodynamics. I didn’t know shit about any of that, so I learned.
> 
> Through that, I concluded the drug I was working on was a dud that needed to be tossed. That shocked a lot of people, because most doctors find it very hard to give up their baby. The drug was meant to go to phase 3, but it had too much variability, and I didn’t think it had the legs. The whole drug class went down in flames. Merck was lucky that we killed it early. Companies like it when you save money and don’t follow everyone else like a lemming into drugs that never work.

**\[Killing a drug headed for phase 3 is a bold move for someone new to a company. How did leadership react?\]**

> Spector did me a huge favor that, at the time, I took as a slap in the face. He said, “We’re moving you to vaccines, and you’re going to develop this new vaccine.” I thought, “What the hell are you doing to me? Why do you hate me so much? I’m a cardiologist, and you’re dumping me in vaccines?”
> 
> I called all of my mentors: ”Oh my god, what am I doing? Maybe I should leave.” Then Spector reminded me, “When you came to Merck, you didn’t come here to be a cardiologist. You came to develop medicines that improve public health and extend people’s lives around the world. You should go where the innovation is. You made that decision, even if you didn’t know it.” I took stock and said, “You’re absolutely right.”
> 
> So I moved to vaccines and found the most important mentor I ever had, John Boslego. He came from Nowheresville, central Pennsylvania. The Army made him. He went to the Uniformed Services University of the Sciences, then Walter Reed, and became a very important vaccine developer. He taught me how to develop vaccines, and more importantly, how to do clinical research on a grand scale. Drugs and vaccines only matter, and you only win, when they get implemented and you see a public health benefit. How do you get there without compromising scientific principles? How do you stage your investment and build a body of evidence so you spend no more and no less than each phase warrants? What will move the needle in public health, and how do you talk to people to find out what it takes for something to be implemented and valued? Through all that, he helped me understand my role.
> 
> My partner in this work was Kathrin Jansen, the basic researcher who developed the vaccine, and together we developed Gardasil and its daughter vaccine, Gardasil 9. One of the most exciting moments came in 2020, when the first papers reported the elimination of cervical cancer in Scandinavia and Australia after mass vaccination with Gardasil.
> 
> Now I run a huge pipeline with a massive budget and 18,000 direct and contract employees. You have to learn how to work within an institution that size. The previous CMO, Roy Baynes, was instrumental there. Every time I grew, I had a mentor who I picked and who picked me. You learn from your mistakes and theirs, and they have to be generous with their time and honest with you.
> 
> Why do they do it? Mostly because they’re building big departments and need people who do very good work, considering that leaders succeed through their people. But most of these folks were also dedicated scientists with a strong belief in public health. They wanted to say, at the end of the day, that they changed public health, not only that they ran a study. Roy Baynes developed Keytruda and changed the face of cancer. He saw cancer deaths drop in the United States because of Keytruda. Now I’m someone else’s mentor, because I’m an old man. I’ve been very lucky to have superstars as mentors.

**3) Vaccines don’t have an easy surrogate endpoint the way LDL does in cardiovascular. What did you learn from your mentors when you started in infectious disease, and what would you tell someone starting in a vaccines group today?**

> Vaccines and infectious disease are a very good lens on the problems of clinical research in general. Viruses are cancer on steroids. They mutate quickly. Every issue around how medical care gets delivered shows up. Vaccines are a touchpoint for how society interacts with medicine, what it prioritizes, and how a country thinks about protecting itself from harm.
> 
> First, you have to understand that a clinical trial is thousands of decisions. Second, you need the foundations of statistics and how to power and build a study. More importantly, you have to understand the condition and people that you are studying. A clinical study is an experiment that lasts years, costs hundreds of millions of dollars, and affects real people. It asks them to undergo many tests, some invasive. It uses products that are still experimental and unlicensed but have to be well characterized and well made. The key is understanding the disease and what will move the needle for decision makers, patients, and regulators.

**\[Gardasil stands out as an example here. Can you walk us through how you designed that program? What did each stakeholder need to see?\]**

> When we started, people knew HPV caused all cervical cancer and some other cancers, and that most infected people never got cancer. In standard vaccine thinking, preventing the infection is all you need to show. If you don’t get the infection, how could you get the disease? HPV didn’t work that way, because OB-GYNs managed this condition. At the time, they didn’t think of it as an infectious disease. They thought of it as a precancerous lesion, because what they saw was cervical cancer itself: Pap abnormalities, CIN 1, 2, and 3, AIS. They saw the annual Pap smear as the vehicle for much of their public health mission.
> 
> To the government however, this was an STD. To a payer the argument had to be made as well: you vaccinate now, and the cancer prevention benefit probably lands with a different insurer 20 or 30 years later. We needed a strong business case for everyone, and we had to build the body of evidence.
> 
> Nobody understood how people got from HPV infection to cancer, or why one person got cancer, and another didn’t. Longitudinal studies would take years, and once someone had an abnormal Pap or colposcopy, the lesion had to be removed. We thought about it for a long time and realized other investigators had already done some of this work. The key was to scour the literature, which AI makes much easier now, and find cohorts where the natural history was already mapped.
> 
> One was at the University of Washington, long a stronghold of STD research. Laura Koutsky had shown that some people develop the immediate precancerous lesion right after HPV infection, and that it sits for years before becoming cancer, probably because a second hit is needed. The precancerous lesion is necessary, but it takes a second hit to reach cancer. That meant if you could prevent CIN 2/3, you could show you prevent cancer, because CIN 3, which included carcinoma in situ, correlated one-to-one with developing cancer. Her research got us that part.
> 
> Then we had to learn how long progression takes and how to follow people for that long. You can’t run a 30-year clinical study, so we looked for people who could. I met a young researcher, Joakim Dillner, who worked with a cancer registry in Sweden, and he introduced me to Steinar Thoresen colleague at the cancer registry of Norway. I learned that in Scandinavia, “you can run but you can’t hide” from the healthcare system. Even in 2000, before the internet was much of anything, people were tracked by their social security numbers for life, by hand and on old computer programs. I went over and met them, and they were humble, lovely people. They asked, “ You really want to use our registry? What for? You can do research with it?” We ended up working with the governments of Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland, and Iceland, through their cancer registries, to follow vaccinated women for life, and eventually men. Finding resourceful tools like that is a big part of the job.
> 
> Then you have to motivate people to enroll. Our participants were young women and men in college. At 18, the last thing on your mind is joining a clinical trial and being followed for years. What would make anyone do that? You have to work out what’s in it for them. We built programs explaining to young women that they would likely have Pap abnormalities in college. For the proof of concept, I suggested approaching schools in the top 10 of the football rankings, on the theory that there must be a lot of risk factors, and that their student health centers might sign on. We learned how to talk to young people, first in the US and then internationally.
> 
> Men were harder to recruit into clinical trials. Women are generally much more responsible about their health than men are, and sampling men for HPV was a scary prospect. You learn to work in the community, figure out which questions you have to answer, how to measure things, and what moves the needle.
> 
> The studies were formative, and they established skill sets around the world for future trials. Brazil enrolled 3,000 of the program’s 50,000 subjects. At the time, patients in Brazil’s public system would line up at big hospitals, sit on a bench until called, and see whichever doctor they got. Cervical screening there wasn’t very effective because the quality was poor. By enrolling, women got the same doctor for five straight years for their HPV and GYN care, an appointment instead of a line, and daycare for their kids. That mattered a lot to them, and it gave us excellent long-term follow-up. We also ran a study in MSM, which needed a very different strategy.
> 
> The bottom line is that clinical trials are about human beings, and about motivating people who are sick or susceptible to take part. You have to design an experiment you don’t run yourself. You hand someone a manual: “here’s what to do, here’s the kit, here are the forms and how to fill them out, here’s how to ship the specimen.” I have never treated a trial subject. We’re not allowed to. So, you have to teach doctors to run the study and keep them motivated when they have a million other things going on. You have to keep subjects motivated and solve every problem at the sites, with patients, and at the central labs. It’s a million little things, constantly. You have to tell a good story and keep it simple.

**\[Many companies hand trials off to CROs, but in some areas like neurology, that has ended up enrolling the wrong patients. How does Merck think about using CROs?\]**

> We never use CROs to run our studies. The people doing the work need real skin in the game. Company people have to understand the study and what’s at stake, and help the sites understand it and stay motivated. Otherwise, you get professional patients and your signal-to-noise decreases dramatically. Clinical trials are blunt instruments. They aren’t precision lab experiments where you control every variable in a dish of cells.

**4) Merck has acquired or licensed a lot of outside programs: sotatercept, Cidara’s flu program, Prometheus, and recently EyeBio’s Wnt pathway drug. When the early proof-of-concept data come from somewhere else, whether a CRO-run trial or an academic center, what signals get you excited? Are there trial design principles you always look for?**

> We do a lot of in-licensing, and we treat it all as one pipeline. Merck Research Laboratories is very productive, but it’s dwarfed by the rest of global drug development. There are hundreds of thousands of compounds out there, and even a company Merck’s size covers only part of the discovery space. So we always look outside.
> 
> We look for high medical need, novelty, and clear proof of biology. You can learn a lot from small studies. You don’t need big ones, but you need the right biomarker and clean, crisp data.
> 
> Take sotatercept, from Acceleron. They focused tightly on activin signaling, an unusual and little-studied pathway. They made great discovery choices and built preclinical models that helped us understand how the drug might work, in models we weren’t sure were relevant but that seemed to be. Then they ran very well-controlled phase 2 studies.
> 
> In phase 2, you try to control as much as you can in the patient group, which unfortunately creates problems in phase 3. Acceleron did something I’ve always seen as a hallmark of success: they had very good sites and a steering committee that understood the disease deeply. With that committee and their own understanding of the drug, they designed a proof-of-concept study called PULSAR, and it was fabulous.
> 
> The phase 2 data looked very good. CROs can become a problem in phase 3, when quality is very hard to maintain. The question for STELLAR, and later HYPERION and ZENITH, was whether phase 3 would reproduce Phase 2, because phase 3 broadens the population to 150 sites that aren’t the same expert centers. But the effect size was large. From phase 2 we concluded this was very important: somewhat higher risk, but high reward. When you have a completely new mechanism that looks disease modifying rather than symptom improving, hits a new node, and shows a lot of corroborating biology in phase 2, you can go forward. Sotatercept turned out to be one of those drugs where phase 3 was more positive than phase 2, because we had underestimated the effect size. Suddenly, you’re reducing serious events that lead to death.
> 
> Prometheus was a different story. It had a strong natural history basis, a lot of genetics behind it, and clear evidence that TL1A was an important node in inflammatory bowel disease. Their study showed good efficacy and potentially a biomarker. Again, these were small studies.
> 
> We look for drugs with a real game-changing opportunity at a new node of biology. TL1A hits inflammation and fibrosis together, in diseases where fibrosis drives the pathophysiology, like Crohn’s or hidradenitis suppurativa. Inflammation is critical but fibrosis is less so in ulcerative colitis.
> 
> With Cidara, the problem is the flu vaccine. It works well one year, terribly the next, okay the year after, because of antigenic drift (the virus changes year on year). Nobody can forecast it well, and in some years efficacy is around 30%, which is nothing. Something more predictable and reliable that isn’t affected by the flu’s classic genetic drift could be a very big deal. It’s as effective as the flu vaccine is in its best years. That’s going to be an important drug, and its phase 3 is coming along.
> 
> It always comes down to the size of the unmet need and whether the drug adds a lot, not a little, to what we can do. With EyeBio, VEGF inhibition has been transformational, but it doesn’t help about 40% of patients with DME. We need something that combines with VEGF, works in VEGF failures, or is orthogonal to it. That’s where Wnt comes in. When the data come out soon, you’ll see a very potent drug with a particular profile. I think it will work swimmingly with VEGF and help the 35% to 40% of patients who don’t respond well to VEGF, whose DME progresses, and who go blind.
> 
> Blindness is horrible. Cancer and pulmonary arterial hypertension are terrible. We’ve never been a cosmetics company. We’re not Botox, though there’s nothing wrong with that. For us, it’s about the major killers, where the biology makes sense, what experiments have been done, and a very careful internal and external analysis of the results. We go from there.

**5) Congratulations on Lipfendra, Merck’s oral PCSK9 inhibitor. It’s another example of Merck partnering early and taking an asset all the way to patients. As a cardiologist, how do you read the recent Lp(a) and IL-6 trials, which set the field back? Was it patient selection, trial design, or targets that aren’t disease-modifying despite the apparent genetic validation?**

> We need to see the phase 3 trials in detail for the LPa and IL-6 trials. Press releases are just press releases. The primary presentations will help, but the secondary presentations will matter most.
> 
> LDL cholesterol is special in terms of risk: it is tightly related to risk of heart attacks and strokes, and we’ve shown that reducing LDL-cholesterol levels reduces risk, and even more, the magnitude of risk reduction of LDL correlates with the magnitude of benefit. This is not true (yet) with any other lipid: because we have found that raising HDL cholesterol doesn’t improve outcomes. Lp(a) has always had quirks in how it relates to cardiovascular risk. LDL is truly causal: the higher it goes, the higher the risk, a monotonic increase. That’s easy. But even taking LDL to zero won’t be enough – you have to do something more. The focus on atherogenic particles makes sense, as does the inflammation some oxidized particles cause.
> 
> You have to be careful with epidemiologic and associational studies. I’m not saying that is the case here, but years ago, epidemiologic studies like the Nurses’ Health Study suggested women on HRT had better cardiovascular outcomes. Those studies were correlative and couldn’t show causation. When a placebo-controlled trial of HRT was run, the women on HRT had a higher risk. In the observational studies, women taking HRT simply had more access to healthcare and got care earlier.
> 
> For Lp(a), we need to take a closer look at the data when they become available. There are a few possibilities. There may be a threshold effect, where only people with very high levels benefit from bringing them down to very low levels. Perhaps the curves haven’t separated yet. With LDL, curves diverge within a few months, but in some diseases the events come later. Inflammation is pleiotropic, and IL-6 may not have been the right node. You also have to avoid clamping down too hard on inflammation, or you start seeing infections.

**\[If IL-6 wasn’t the right node, are there other inflammatory targets you find more promising? NLRP3, for example, has been pitched for everything from cardiovascular disease to Parkinson’s.\]**

> Everyone has talked about NLRP3 for everything that ails you. What I find fascinating is that SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1s are what have moved the needle outside of atherogenic particles. Energy metabolism and sugar control make a big difference across the disease spectrum. When you see such beautiful effect sizes on cardiovascular outcomes in patients on GLP-1s, you start to wonder whether we’ve been barking up the wrong tree.
> 
> Colchicine and the CANTOS trial gave us good data that treating inflammation can reduce cardiovascular events. But inflammation markers like hsCRP don’t track outcomes one-to-one the way LDL does, so we still don’t know which marker to follow or which part of the pathway to hit. There’s a lot of work ahead. Lp(a) has the same open question. *Pelacarsen* did what it was supposed to do and dropped Lp(a) by 86%, and I can’t wait to see the full outcomes trial, probably at AHA. That’s very nice, but science is hard. Lowering a marker doesn’t guarantee fewer heart attacks.

**\[CRISPR approaches are very interesting, but when you talk to patients coming into clinic, would they actually undergo CRISPR therapy in 2026?\]**

> Patients won’t take a CRISPR therapy until it becomes “normal”. Until then, anyone who can get an 80-plus percent LDL drop from a daily oral regimen at a relatively low price should take that route instead. CRISPR will be great for sickle cell disease, some cancers, and perhaps disease modification in conditions like amyloid deposition. However, it’s a long road ahead to get there, and the pricing will be complicated.

**6) Lately we’ve seen many policy changes, from the IRA to the recent shifts at HHS and FDA. On the ground, have policies helped or hurt programs and their timelines?**

> The current situation in American healthcare, and more broadly in global healthcare, is problematic. Every system has its own trade-offs and inefficiencies. In the United States, the biggest problem is that 50% of every drug dollar goes to middlemen, the PBMs, who add very little beyond some profit for their companies. Meanwhile, American patients can’t afford their medicines. On top of that, patients also face the crippling cost of hospitalization.
> 
> The IRA has been a real headwind for Pharma drug development because it discriminates against pills. Biologics get 13 years on the market before a Medicare-negotiated price kicks in, while small molecules get only 9. As it stands, that makes biologics much more attractive and makes multi-indication development very hard. Keytruda was approved around 2014, and we are still reading out clinical studies in additional indications. That model wouldn’t work anymore. You have to either develop a drug for a small set of indications or take enormous risks running many indications in parallel, which is very challenging.
> 
> To be honest, the pricing situation isn’t great. I understand the goal of saving money, but if you cut out the PBMs or limit their markups, you’d save American taxpayers and patients a lot of money. Price controls make it harder and harder to take risks. That’s just how it is. Companies end up chasing incremental benefits and me-too drugs, because betting on something truly new becomes dangerous. It distorts the market. Additionally, there’s instability at HHS. If policy changes every year, it’s very hard to plan for a drug that takes 9 to 12 years to develop.

**7) With sac-TMT and the PD-1/VEGF bispecifics, you’re watching Chinese data arrive in real time, sometimes from a partner and sometimes from a competitor. How have you handled that as CMO? From your perspective, where is the US still best positioned for innovation relative to China?**

> First, we have to recognize that the Chinese government and people wanted better outcomes for patients in China. Second, the government made a concerted push to lead in many industries, including biotech. They upgraded their capabilities. China’s regulatory body used to have very long timelines. Then, they decided to become a world-class regulator: clear, reliable, and friendly to novel research. They built the infrastructure for fast first-in-human studies and consistent, clear communication with sponsors. This makes life much easier for anyone doing novel research.
> 
> They also upgraded clinical trial sites, which top-down direction makes possible. To be called a research hospital in China, you must know how to run clinical trials, pass good clinical practice inspections, and be very good at it. They do constant inspections to keep hospitals at the top of their game, and running phase 3 trials has to be part of the mission. Those efforts have built real capacity for drug development. Those upgrades also have downstream effects: many Chinese nationals who trained in the US and Europe took those learnings home and said, “We can do this here, and we can beat you.”
> 
> China’s capabilities stem from this confluence: 1) a strong regulator determined to be as unbureaucratic as possible and to encourage novelty and clinical research, 2) a regime that makes hospitals comply with GCP if they want to be research centers, and 3) very large investments into biotech in a country of 1.3 billion people. This was a 25-year-long investment, and naturally, a booming industry followed.
> 
> With Chinese assets, every mechanism of action has multiple versions, so you can’t just walk in and pick up the winning drug. You have to figure out which is best. Chinese biotechs offer you 25 flavors of PD-1/VEGF, for example, and you need to decide which has the best profile. Studies also run faster there because of the country’s size, regulatory friendliness, and big hospitals.
> 
> As for the US: FDA needs to improve its regulatory fitness. It has started with a program for phase 1 trials called Trial Blazer. This is great, but it needs to do more. NCI-designated cancer centers need to see their mission as bringing cancer drugs to patients in two or three years, through phase 3 trials, as well as in 20 years through basic research. Further, the government and hospitals need to encourage trial participation. Right now, many patients are penalized for joining trials because of the effect on various kinds of insurance, and there’s no coordinated push to get people to participate. The US can do a lot to keep its lead.
> 
> We’re still ahead, but China is coming very fast. They’re doing it out of competitiveness, not malice. They want to do for biotech what they’ve done for electric cars everywhere but the US. Where there’s a will, thebre’s a way. Americans do one thing best: innovate. We need to unlock that innovation by deregulating and by creating incentives for people to join clinical trials.

**8) How has Merck been using AI, and where has it made the biggest difference?**

> We use AI a lot in drug discovery now, and we’re starting to apply it in clinical research. Much of clinical research is back-office work, especially data management: moving data from vendors and labs into the main database, analyzing trends, and catching sites that are getting the study plan wrong. AI can do all of that, and it’s going to transform how we run trials.
